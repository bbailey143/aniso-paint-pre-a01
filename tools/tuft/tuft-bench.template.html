Filling the tuft

A tuft here is a shell. The round brush puts every hair on one circle and leaves the middle empty; the flat puts every hair on one straight line between its two spines, two deep. Both are perfectly regular, and both are identical on every stroke ever painted. This page keeps the recorded spines and rebuilds the hairs on top of them as a filled bundle with randomness in it — so the two can be looked at side by side with one thing changed.

The spines are engine output, recorded with brush-bench tuft. The hairs are the proposal, generated live, and every dial below is real.

Three things the recording says outright

Both of these came out of the carrier file before any of the above was built. Neither is an opinion about how a brush ought to work.

  • The second spine is a copy Over all 49 frames of a press-and-pull, for both flats, max |x₀−x₁| = 0.000000 and max |z₀−z₁| = 0.000000. The two spines differ only by the fixed ±half-width they were placed at. They see the same ferrule, the same drag, the same friction direction and the same floor, so they solve to the same curve every time. A flat brush has one spine's worth of behaviour, and the surface between them can only ever be a straight ruled sheet — no corner leads, nothing buckles, nothing twists.
  • One of the round's hairs is a duplicate The ring is laid out as u = b / (B−1), angle u × 2π. Hair 0 lands at 0 and hair 27 lands at 2π, which is the same place. 28 bristles, 27 positions — one hair is drawn twice, lays paint twice and costs twice, forever. Small, but it is the kind of thing a ring hides and a filled section cannot.
  • The flats are a diffraction grating Spacing between neighbouring hair tracks across the blade, at working pressure: 0% variation for the flat sable and 0% for the flat hog. Not approximately even — exactly even, to every decimal place, on every stroke. A perfectly regular comb dragged across a textured surface is the classic way to manufacture a periodic pattern, and a periodic pattern in the paint is the artefact that has been coming back all week. The round sable is 72%, which is only because a ring projected onto a line gives uneven spacing by accident.

What the fill measures out at

Coverage is how much of the mark's own outline is actually inked, rasterised at 0.15 cells and clipped to the outline so the rim band cannot flatter it. Live numbers on each card; these are the defaults, at frame 20, full press.

  • Round sable 26% → 88%The empty middle is the whole of that gap. 28 hairs on a circle ink a rim; 96 through the disc ink the disc.
  • Flat sable 30% → 76%A two-deep ribbon against a chisel section with real thickness.
  • Flat hog 39% → 80%And its stray bristles now reach outside the mark, which is most of what a hog looks like.
  • Hair count is freeSwept 40 to 180 hairs: coverage stayed between 65% and 90% at every count, because a drawn hair stands for a bundle and its thickness follows the packing rather than the count. So count is chosen on cost and on how fine the striations should be — not to fix the hollow. The hollow is fixed by where the roots sit.

The rows this would add

Chosen, not measured — the same standing the brush library's existing numbers have. Each one is a sentence about the tool: a sable is dressed hair that points, a hog is coarse bristle cut blunt.

Still hollow, in the part this does not touch

Worth being straight about what a fill cannot buy on its own.

  • Every hair still rides one curveThe bundle is filled, but its shape is still a blend of the same one or two solved spines. Per-hair stiffness spreads them apart — soft hairs lie over further than the spine, stiff ones do not reach it — and that is a real spread, but it is a spread about one curve, not hairs finding their own way. Two spines that solve identically cannot give a flat brush a leading corner.
  • The clamp at the sheet is crudeA hair pushed below the surface is set to z = 0 where it stands. A real hair would lie along the surface and lengthen its contact. This is why soft hairs stay down through a stroke here, which is the behaviour wanted, but it is bought with an approximation and not with a contact solve.
  • Splay is still geometricsplayFromPressure multiplies the section by a number derived from the contact count. Press harder, the whole bundle scales. Nothing fans because it met a ridge.
  • The release is still a teleportUnchanged from the last recording: end() does not move the tuft, and one hover solve snaps it straight. Scrub past frame 34 with either tuft drawn and it is the same single-frame jump.
